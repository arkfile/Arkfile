# Terms and Privacy Pages (Initial Considerations)

This note is not legal advice. It records how to start drafting public Terms of Service and Privacy Policy pages for a hosted instance such as arkfile.net so those pages match how Arkfile actually works, stay honest, and cover the usual expectations under US, UK, and EU law without pretending the service is something it is not.

## What these pages are for

The website pages should be instance law for arkfile.net, not a rewrite of `docs/privacy.md` or `docs/security.md`. Those files are technical and operator-facing. The site needs two short public pages: a Privacy Policy (what you collect, why, and what people can ask for) and Terms of Service (the contract for using the hosted service). A third Acceptable Use page is optional; for a first pass the rules can live inside the Terms. The AGPLv3 license already covers the software. Do not mix that license into the hosted-service contract except to say that source is available and that running your own instance is a different operator with different terms.

The first drafting decision is who the user is contracting with. GDPR, UK GDPR, and California-style notices all start with a named controller: legal name, a contact address, and a country. "Arkfile" and "arkfile.net" are product names, not a legal person. Until that is filled in, the pages are templates. The same pages should say they apply only to the arkfile.net instance. Self-hosted copies should not inherit this operator's identity, storage vendors, or payment destinations.

Write both documents in the same plain voice as the user FAQ, and under-claim rather than over-claim. The honest posture is already in the developer docs: files, filenames, content hashes, password hints, tags, and passwords stay off the server in usable form; the server still sees username, encrypted size, chunk layout, public salts, password type, ownership, timestamps, and billing fields. Optional contact details (email, Signal, and so on) are encrypted at rest but readable by the administrator. Share recipients need no account, but unauthenticated rate limiting still processes IP address, User-Agent, and Accept-Language in memory to make a daily-rotating Entity ID. Authenticated security events can store a username. There is no analytics stack in the current design, and session cookies are first-party and functional. If the Privacy page says "we never see personal data" or "fully anonymous," it will be false, and that is worse than a shorter, accurate list.

## Privacy Policy

For UK and EU law, structure the Privacy page around the Article 13 information duties, written as prose rather than a compliance checklist. Name the controller and how to reach them. List categories of data in three buckets. First, account and operations data the operator can actually read: username, approval status, MFA enrollment records, storage accounting, credit ledger, timestamps, and encrypted-but-admin-readable contact info if the user provided it. Second, ciphertext the operator stores but cannot decrypt: file blobs, owner metadata, and share envelopes. Third, transient or derived security data: Entity IDs, security-event rows, and journald operational logs without stored IPs.

Give a purpose and a lawful basis for each. Contract is the natural basis for running the account, storing ciphertext, issuing session cookies, and billing credits. Legitimate interests is the usual basis for rate limiting, abuse detection, and security events. Consent is only needed if optional marketing or non-essential cookies are added later. A cookie banner should not be needed if the instance stays on the current `__Host-` session and CSRF cookies only (`__Host-arkfile-token`, `__Host-arkfile-temp`, `__Host-arkfile-refresh`, `__Host-arkfile-csrf`). Those cookies are first-party, Secure, SameSite=Strict, and used only to keep a login session and prevent cross-site request forgery.

Say clearly that the operator cannot fulfill an access or portability request by handing over plaintext files or a forgotten password, because the operator does not have them. What can be provided is the account record, operational metadata, and any contact info on file. Erasure is the awkward one. Users can delete files themselves, but account deletion today looks admin-initiated, and billing rows are kept for ledger integrity. The Privacy page should say how someone asks for deletion, what is removed (ciphertext, shares, MFA, contact blob), and what may be retained for a stated period (credit history, security events, legal holds).

Name recipients: the hosting VPS, the S3-compatible storage vendor, DNS and TLS providers, and any email provider used for support. If storage or hosting is outside the UK or EU, say that transfers happen and that the instance relies on the vendor's transfer mechanism (standard contractual clauses or equivalent). Give retention in plain numbers once those numbers are decided, for example security events for a defined window, session cookies for 30 minutes and 7 days, and contact info until the user deletes it or the account is closed. Point to the ICO or the user's local authority for complaints.

For children, UK and EU information-society rules cluster around 13 to 16 depending on country. COPPA in the US is 13. There is no age gate in the product today. A conservative first policy is 18 and over, or 16 and over with a parent if the operator wants to be more open, and then that rule should actually be stated at registration if it is claimed.

US coverage can stay in the same Privacy page instead of a second California-only document. List the same categories, say the operator does not sell or share personal information for cross-context advertising, describe retention, and say California and similar-state residents can request access, correction, and deletion of the data the operator actually holds. Because the instance does not run ads or a sale of data, it does not need a "Do Not Sell" toggle. COPPA only matters if under-13 accounts are allowed; they should not be.

Do not paste `docs/privacy.md` onto the website. It is too technical, and it still understates that contact info and operational metadata are personal data.

## Terms of Service

The Terms are the contract and the liability shield, written so a consumer in the UK or EU is not being sold unenforceable small-print. Describe the service as a hosted encrypted vault and share mechanism, with admin approval before full use, required MFA, and no password reset. Put the non-custodial facts in the contract itself: lost password means lost files; the operator cannot decrypt; admin-assisted MFA reset is a last resort that depends on out-of-band identity checks and optional contact info.

Users are responsible for what they upload and for how they share (URL plus share password). Because the operator cannot inspect ciphertext, the Terms still need a rule that the operator may suspend or delete an account or object if they receive a credible legal notice, a court order, or evidence of abuse at the account or share-ID layer. That is how the pages stay honest under the US DMCA and notice model, the UK Online Safety Act's user-to-user expectations, and the EU Digital Services Act hosting-provider duties. The operator is not claiming to moderate file contents. The operator is claiming they will act on identifiers they can see. Ban the obvious categories (malware distribution, child sexual abuse material, terrorist content, unauthorized access, and using the service to attack others) without pretending files are scanned.

State that share pages are public to anyone with the link, that envelope metadata (`share_id`, `file_id`, salt, encrypted envelope, and `size_bytes`) is fetchable before the password is entered, and that the owner is responsible for password strength and how the password is delivered.

Payments need their own short section if arkfile.net is taking money. Credits, storage quotas, and manual Bitcoin or Monero top-ups are not a card checkout. Say credits are prepaid store credit, applied manually after confirmation, not instant, and generally non-refundable except where consumer law requires otherwise. UK and EU paid consumers have stronger cancellation and unfair-term protections than a US "as is" clause; do not write a US-only waiver and hope it travels. Crypto also raises a separate question that should not be buried only in website copy: whether taking BTC or XMR as payment for a hosted service creates money-transmission or AML duties in the operator's home jurisdiction. The website can stay factual (manual on-chain top-up, no automated processor) while that point is checked separately.

Limitation of liability should match the security docs. The software is described as having no formal certifications. The browser client is trusted only if the origin is honest; a compromised host can serve a bad client. Availability is best-effort, not an SLA. Cap damages at fees paid in a recent period if the instance charges, and do not promise that encryption makes the service suitable for every high-risk use. Consumer law in the UK and EU will still block exclusion of death, personal injury, or fraud, and will limit how far goods or services that are not as described can be disclaimed. Keep the disclaimer, but keep it lawful.

## Placement and tone

Put both pages at stable URLs (`/privacy.html`, `/terms.html`), link them in every footer next to FAQ and Contact Admin, and add a one-line acknowledgment at registration ("I agree to the Terms and have read the Privacy Policy") rather than a scroll-wrap theater. Date the documents and say they may be updated with notice on the site. Keep the first drafts short: a few screens of Privacy and a few screens of Terms, written like the FAQ, with no cookie banner unless analytics are added later. A solicitor in the operator's home country should read the payment, liability, and "we cannot decrypt" sections before they go live. The product facts are already strong enough that the legal pages mostly need to say them in the first person, name a controller, and not promise anonymity the service does not have.

## Open questions

These answers will decide the parts generic text cannot invent. Who is the legal person behind arkfile.net (individual, sole trader, Ltd, LLC), in which country, and what postal or privacy-contact address can be published? Which storage backend and VPS region does production actually use today? Should the pages be arkfile.net-only, or also a fill-in template for self-hosters? What minimum age should be stated, and is the operator willing to add a registration checkbox for it? Is account deletion something a user can request by email only, or is self-serve delete wanted later? How long should security events, admin logs, and credit ledgers be kept after an account is closed? Are crypto top-ups live on production, and is any fiat taken as well? Should governing law be UK/England, a US state, or a split (UK and EU consumers keep mandatory local rights; everyone else uses the operator's home court)? Is the operator comfortable stating, in public, that they cannot produce plaintext files to law enforcement and that they will only produce the operational record plus any admin-readable contact info?
